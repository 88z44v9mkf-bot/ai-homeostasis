# 2. Related work

**Human fatigue and protective disengagement.** Cognitive-energetical accounts describe performance under sustained load as a compensatory trade-off: as resources deplete, the operator protects the primary task by withdrawing effort from secondary channels, narrowing attention, and accepting lower accuracy [@hockey1997; @kahneman1973]. Resilience engineering frames the same behavior as an efficiency-thoroughness trade-off: under pressure, a system becomes more efficient and less thorough, and the question is whether it knows when to stop [@hollnagel2009]. Our work imports this lens into LLM agents, but keeps it functional: we measure behavior, not experience.

**Long context and attention.** Transformer attention is quadratic in context length, and long contexts are used unevenly. "Lost in the middle" shows that models retrieve information at the beginning and end of a long context better than in the middle [@liu2024lost; @vaswani2017attention]. This is a concrete mechanism for a "zoning out" pattern: the input is present, but not attended. COGLOAD tests whether this positional effect becomes a reliability failure under load and whether a homeostatic protocol mitigates it.

**Hallucination, abstention, and calibration.** LLMs often produce fluent but unsupported claims [@ji2023hallucination; @bender2021]. Calibration work shows that models have partial internal knowledge of what they know [@kadavath2022], and semantic entropy can detect hallucination [@farquhar2024]. Our self-report measure is intentionally simple: we ask the model for a load rating and test whether that rating predicts its own success. The finding that it does not is a calibration result in a load context.

**Agent reliability and benchmarks.** Agent benchmarks measure task success in web, software, and general environments [@mialon2023gaia; @zhou2023webarena; @jimenez2023swebench]. They typically assume the runtime is healthy. COGLOAD varies the health of the runtime and asks what happens to the agent's behavior, which is complementary: the same task can be easy or impossible depending on load, and a good agent should know the difference.

**Graceful degradation in systems.** Queueing theory gives the vocabulary of load, capacity, and latency [@kleinrock1975]; software systems use timeouts, circuit breakers, load shedding, and backpressure. GCP-style commerce protocols add typed errors and recovery paths. COGLOAD tests whether the <i>model</i>, not only the surrounding system, participates in that regulation, and compares model-initiated protection with engineering protection.

**Positioning.** Prior work studies long-context retrieval, hallucination, or agent success separately. This paper studies the interaction: under load, does the agent's behavior shift from "trying to answer" to "silently dropping inputs," and can a small amount of protocol restore honest degradation? The contribution is the frame, the benchmark, and the finding, not a claim that models are conscious of fatigue.
